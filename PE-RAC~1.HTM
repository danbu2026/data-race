Who Gained, Who Lost — 2015 vs 2025

In 2015, Blackstone controlled 31% of the combined AUM of these 8 firms. By 2025 its share had actually fallen to 27% — even as its absolute AUM grew 3.8x — because Apollo and Ares grew faster. The race within the race: who is eating whose lunch.

Key Moments That Shaped the Race

JUNE 2007
Blackstone IPO — private equity goes public
Steve Schwarzman rang the bell at the NYSE with $70 billion in AUM. The Blackstone IPO was controversial — was it the top of the buyout market? Sceptics were proven spectacularly wrong. Going public gave Blackstone permanent capital, brand credibility, and a currency to pay for talent and acquisitions that private firms could not match.
SEP 2008
Lehman Brothers collapses — PE fundraising freezes
The Global Financial Crisis devastated leveraged buyouts. PE firms couldn't raise debt, couldn't exit investments, and portfolio companies struggled. But the crisis also revealed a strategic advantage: firms with dry powder — KKR, Apollo, Carlyle — could buy distressed assets at historic discounts. The crisis accelerated the shift to credit strategies that now dominate PE growth.
2012
Carlyle and Apollo IPOs — a new model takes hold
Both Carlyle ($147B AUM) and Apollo ($113B AUM) went public within months of each other. The message to the industry was clear: permanent capital, public brand visibility, and institutional-grade governance were competitive advantages. The "partnership" model that PE firms had guarded for decades began to look like a liability.
2014
Ares IPO — the credit revolution begins
Ares Management listed with $74B in AUM, less than half the size of Carlyle. What the market underestimated was Ares's credit-first DNA. As banks retreated from corporate lending post-Dodd-Frank, Ares filled the vacuum. By 2025 it had grown 8x in 11 years — the fastest AUM growth rate of any firm in this race — largely through direct lending and alternative credit.
JAN 2021
Apollo-Athene merger — insurance changes everything
Apollo completed its acquisition of Athene, a retirement services company with $200B in insurance assets. Overnight, Apollo's AUM jumped by nearly $80 billion. KKR had done the same with Global Atlantic. This wasn't just dealmaking — it was a fundamental shift in how PE firms source capital. Insurance float is permanent, low-cost, and unaffected by fundraising cycles. The firms that paired with insurers built a structurally different business.
JAN 2022
TPG IPO — the last major PE firm goes public
TPG's Nasdaq IPO at $9B valuation completed a 15-year transformation of the PE industry from private partnerships to public alternative asset managers. With $109B in AUM at IPO, TPG then acquired Angelo Gordon in 2023 for $2.7B, immediately adding $50B+ in AUM. The acquisition playbook — buy scale rather than raise it — has become standard.
NOV 2023
Blackstone crosses $1 trillion in AUM
No asset manager in history had reached $1 trillion in private markets. Blackstone did it with a model that would have been unrecognisable in 2005: retail investor access through vehicles like BREIT, insurance capital from reinsurers, and a $70B+ credit business. The firm had evolved from a buyout shop into a multi-asset manager that resembles BlackRock more than it resembles the original PE model.
2024
CVC IPO — Europe's PE giant goes public
CVC Capital Partners listed on Euronext Amsterdam, completing the final major PE IPO of the era. With €200B in AUM and the world's largest-ever PE fund (€26B raised in 2023), CVC's listing was the European answer to the Wall Street PE machine. It also confirmed a structural truth: in the modern PE industry, being private is now the exception, not the rule.

What This Means for Finance Professionals

Private equity is no longer "alternative"

In 2005, these 8 firms managed a combined ~$220 billion — roughly the size of one large bank's trading book. By 2025, they manage nearly $4.7 trillion. This is larger than the GDP of Germany. Private equity has become a core part of the global financial system, not a niche strategy. Every finance professional now works in an industry that is partly shaped by PE capital, even if they never work directly for a PE firm.

The credit pivot is the biggest story most people missed

Ares grew 8x in 11 years not through buyouts — but through direct lending, CLOs, and alternative credit. Apollo's insurance strategy is fundamentally a fixed-income management business. KKR's fastest-growing segment is infrastructure and credit. The PE industry's dirty secret is that its biggest growth has come from replacing banks as credit providers — a structural shift driven by post-GFC regulation that most traditional finance professionals still haven't fully absorbed.

Carlyle's lag is a leadership story, not a strategy story

In 2005, Carlyle was arguably more prestigious than Blackstone. It had three famous founders, government connections on every continent, and a brand built on geopolitical deal-making. By 2025, it manages $480B against Blackstone's $1.27T — less than 40 cents for every dollar Blackstone manages. The divergence tracks almost exactly to Carlyle's founder succession issues (2017–2022), while Blackstone and Apollo had stable, execution-focused leadership. Culture and continuity compound just like capital does.

Risk: global Atlantic integration complexity if credit markets stress. Still on track.
🟢 Bullish — insurance + infrastructure combination
Ares — credit growth has structural tailwinds
Direct lending, CLOs, and real assets credit are all benefiting from bank retreat. Ares is less dependent on buyout cycles than peers. At current trajectory, $1T is possible by 2028. Downside: a credit cycle turn hits direct lending harder than equity strategies. Base case: $800B by end 2027. Most underrated firm in this race.
🟢 Bullish — least cyclical of the group
Carlyle — needs a catalyst
Leadership has stabilised under Harvey Schwartz. Credit expansion is the priority. But Carlyle lacks an insurance partner (unlike Apollo and KKR) and its retail channel is less developed than Blackstone's. Base case: $600B by end 2027, organic growth only. Upside: acquires a credit platform or insurance partnership to close the structural gap with peers.
🟡 Neutral — solid but structurally disadvantaged
TPG — integration phase, then acceleration
Angelo Gordon integration is the near-term focus. TPG's technology and climate investing differentiation is a genuine long-term edge as ESG-aligned capital grows. Base case: $400B by 2027. Downside: tech valuations compress, weakening flagship fund performance. Growth story is real but execution risk is higher than peers.
🟡 Neutral — compelling strategy, high execution bar
CVC — European expansion opportunity
Public listing provides acquisition currency. European private credit market is underdeveloped versus the US — CVC is positioned to replicate the US direct lending build-out. Risk: European economic slowdown, political risk. Base case: €270B by 2027. The IPO was the starting gun, not the finish line.
🟡 Bullish in Europe — less certain globally
Warburg Pincus — niche strength, capped scale
Deliberately not trying to be a mega-manager. Asia focus (particularly India and Southeast Asia) provides differentiated deal flow. But without insurance partnerships, retail channels, or credit expansion, growth is capped by traditional fundraising cycles. Base case: $100B by 2028. The most "traditional" PE firm in this race, for better and worse.
🔴 Cautious — deliberate scale ceiling limits growth

🤖 The Real Disruption: AI Is Coming for the PE Playbook

Private equity built a $4.7 trillion industry on information asymmetry. PE firms found deals that public markets couldn't see, hired consultants that portfolio companies couldn't afford, and ran operational improvement programs at a scale that family-owned businesses couldn't replicate. The edge was people, relationships, and proprietary process — things that took decades to build and couldn't be copied overnight.

AI threatens every part of that edge. Deal sourcing that took analyst pools of 50 people is being automated. Due diligence that once filled data rooms with PDFs can now be processed in hours. Portfolio monitoring dashboards that required dedicated finance teams can run continuously on structured feeds. The firms that are investing in AI infrastructure now — Blackstone has announced a dedicated AI team, Apollo is deploying AI in insurance claims processing — are building a new kind of edge to replace the old one.

For the finance professional watching this chart: the real question is not which firm wins the AUM race. The question is what role you want to play in the next iteration of it. The analysts and associates who spend 2026 learning how to use AI tools in financial modelling, due diligence, and reporting will be more valuable to these firms than the ones who are waiting for the tools to stabilise. This chart took days of research to build in the old way. It took hours with AI. That efficiency gap is exactly what is reshaping every industry in this chart.

In 2005, the combined AUM of these 8 firms was ~$220 billion. By 2025, it was $4.7 trillion — a 21x increase in 20 years. The next 20 years will be shaped by whoever combines institutional capital with AI-powered deal intelligence most effectively. Which firm do you think wins — and where do you want to be when they do?
