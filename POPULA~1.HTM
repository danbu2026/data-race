Data Sources & Methodology

This visualisation stitches together data from four primary sources across three distinct eras. Each era uses the most reliable measurement standard available at the time.

For 1995–2008, the gold standard is Comscore Media Metrix, which measured monthly unique visitors via a panel of real users — the same data used by media buyers. The mid-1990s keyframes are anchored to known published Comscore data points with linear interpolation between them.

For 2008–2015, Alexa Internet (owned by Amazon until 2022) provided daily traffic rankings cross-referenced with Comscore quarterly reports. From 2015 onward, SimilarWeb became the industry standard, combining ISP data, browser extensions, and direct measurement. Statista and StatCounter provide corroboration.

Transparency note: Monthly visit figures are total visits (not unique users). One user visiting Google 20 times counts as 20 visits — this is why Google's numbers (~83B/month in early 2026) look far larger than Facebook's. Mobile app traffic (the majority of Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok usage) is not fully captured here, which under-counts those platforms relative to their true reach.

The Five Eras of the Web

1995 – 2001
AOL Era
Dial-up dominance. AOL's free CD campaign built a captive portal audience. Yahoo rises as the web's first directory/search hybrid.
2001 – 2007
Portal Wars
Yahoo vs Google. MSN/Hotmail powers Microsoft's share. GeoCities fades. MySpace briefly becomes the social king.
2007 – 2013
Social Takeover
Facebook and YouTube explode. Google cements #1. Twitter emerges. Smartphones begin reshaping traffic patterns.
2013 – 2020
The Big Three
Google, Facebook, YouTube lock in the top 3 for nearly a decade. Instagram, Amazon, and Reddit steadily climb.
2020 – 2026
AI Disruption
TikTok surges. ChatGPT launches Nov 2022 and reaches top-5 globally by 2025 — the fastest rise of any website in history.

Major Trends Identified

Search always wins traffic. Google's dominance from 2004 onward reflects a structural truth: search is the gateway to everything else. Every time someone wants to go anywhere on the web, they often pass through Google first. This "tollbooth" position makes Google's visit counts structurally higher than any destination site.

The death of the portal. AOL, Yahoo, MSN, Lycos, Excite — all were portals that tried to be everything. All peaked and declined as users switched to purpose-built destinations. Yahoo's refusal to sell to Microsoft in 2008 for $44.6B (it later sold for $4.8B in 2017) is the clearest illustration of this decline.

Video is gravity. YouTube has held a top-2 or top-3 global position since ~2010 and shows no sign of slipping. 500 hours of video are uploaded every minute. The combination of professional, amateur, and algorithmic content makes it essentially impossible to displace.

Social media plateaued in the 2020s. Facebook's web traffic peaked around 2020–2021 and has been declining since — partly because app usage dominates, partly due to demographic shifts toward TikTok and Instagram.

ChatGPT is the biggest story. No website in history has grown faster. From launch (Nov 2022) to 1 billion monthly visits took less than 5 months. It reached ~5.9B visits/month by late 2025 — a trajectory that rivalled Google's annual growth happening in single quarters.

The Big Picture

The 31-year arc of this chart tells a single story: whoever controls the gateway controls the web. AOL controlled the gateway via software. Yahoo controlled it via the browser bookmark. Google controlled it via the search box. And now AI assistants are attempting to become the new gateway — the place where users start their digital journey instead of a search bar.

The question for the 2030s is whether Google can successfully make this transition (its Gemini integration into Search suggests it is trying), or whether a new gateway — ChatGPT, a voice assistant, an OS-level AI — displaces it the same way Google displaced Yahoo. History suggests disruption is a matter of when, not if.
